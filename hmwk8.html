HMWK 8 — Comparative Essay

Bernoulli Processes, Binomial Structure & Combinatorial Links

Goal

In this homework I discuss the analogies (and key differences) between the Law of Large Numbers (LLN) simulation of HMWK 4 and the random-walk / binomial experiment of HMWK 7. I then connect both to classical combinatorial structures: binomial coefficients, Pascal’s triangle, the binomial expansion, and their relationships (including the link to the Fibonacci sequence via shallow diagonals).

1) Bernoulli process as the common core

  • HMWK 4 (LLN). I simulated i.i.d. Bernoulli(p) trials and tracked the relative frequency f(n)=K/n, with K successes in n trials. The LLN states that f(n) → p as n → ∞.
  • HMWK 7 (Random walk). I modeled weekly server security as steps X_t∈{+1,−1}. With secure-week probability q=(1−p)^m, the cumulative score is Sₙ = ∑ X_t = 2K − n, where K ~ Binomial(n,q).

Same Bernoulli engine, different observables: HMWK 4 focuses on the mean (relative frequency), while HMWK 7 focuses on the sum (random walk). One is centered on convergence to p; the other on the full distribution of possible totals (Binomial) and its mapping to walk endpoints.

2) Similarities and differences

  • Similarities. Independent Bernoulli trials; counts of success K are Binomial(n, p or n, q); for large n both exhibit Gaussian-like behavior by CLT.
  • Differences.
    • Quantity observed: f(n)=K/n (LLN) vs. Sₙ=2K−n (random walk).
    • Convergence notion: LLN → f(n)→p; random walk → the distribution of Sₙ is Binomially induced.
    • Drift: in HMWK 7, E[Sₙ]=n(2q−1), so the walk has upward/downward drift unless q=1/2.

3) Binomial coefficients & Pascal’s triangle

The number of ways to obtain exactly K=k successes in n Bernoulli trials is the binomial coefficient C(n,k)=n!/(k!(n−k)!). These coefficients form Pascal’s triangle, where each entry equals the sum of the two above it.

Mini Pascal (rows 0–6)

n=0:                         1
n=1:                       1   1
n=2:                     1   2   1
n=3:                   1   3   3   1
n=4:                 1   4   6   4   1
n=5:               1   5  10  10   5   1
n=6:             1   6  15  20  15   6   1

Each row n provides all the C(n,k) for k=0..n, which weight the probabilities in the Binomial distribution and count the number of random-walk paths that end at a given sum.

4) Binomial expansion & probability mass function

The Binomial theorem states (a+b)^n = ∑k=0..n C(n,k) a^{n−k} b^k. Setting a=q and b=(1−q) gives 1 = (q + (1−q))^n = ∑ C(n,k) q^k (1−q)^{n−k}, i.e. the probabilities over all k sum to 1.

5) Random-walk paths counted by coefficients

A length-n walk with steps +1 (secure) and −1 (breach) reaches Sₙ = 2k−n exactly when there are k upward steps. The number of distinct paths with that endpoint is C(n,k). This is the same coefficient appearing in:

  • the combinatorial count of sequences with k successes,
  • the probabilistic weight in Binomial(n,q),
  • the algebraic coefficient in the expansion of (q + (1−q))^n.

6) Shallow diagonals & Fibonacci numbers

Summing the shallow diagonals of Pascal’s triangle yields the Fibonacci sequence: F₀=0, F₁=1, F₂=1, F₃=2, F₄=3, F₅=5, … In combinatorial terms, F_{n} counts certain restricted binary strings or constrained paths. One classic identity is F_{n} = ∑ C(n−1−k, k) (sum over feasible k), which can be visualized as a sum along “shallow” diagonals in Pascal.

7) Moments, scaling & limits

  • LLN (HMWK 4): E[f(n)] = p and Var[f(n)] = p(1−p)/n → dispersion shrinks as 1/n.
  • Random walk (HMWK 7): with weekly secure prob q, E[Sₙ]=n(2q−1), Var[Sₙ]=4n q(1−q). After centering and scaling by √n, the distribution tends to Normal (CLT).

8) Takeaways

  • Both HMWK 4 and HMWK 7 are different “views” of the same Bernoulli mechanism: the average (frequency) versus the sum (walk).
  • Binomial coefficients are the combinatorial backbone: they count sequences, weight probabilities, and build Pascal’s triangle and the binomial expansion.
  • Classical sequences (e.g., Fibonacci) emerge from the same triangle via diagonal sums, highlighting deep links between probability, algebra, and combinatorics.
