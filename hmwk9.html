HMWK 9 — Conceptual Essay

Probability Interpretations, Measure-Theoretic Foundations & Axiomatic Properties

Goal

In this homework I:

  • review the main interpretations of probability (classical, frequentist, Bayesian, geometric, axiomatic);
  • explain how the axiomatic approach reconciles the apparent conceptual conflicts among them;
  • summarize the link between probability theory and measure theory (σ-algebras, measures, random variables);
  • derive, from the axioms alone, the subadditivity property and the inclusion–exclusion principle.

1) Main Interpretations of Probability

1.1 Classical Interpretation (Laplace)

Historically, the first systematic interpretation of probability is the classical one, attributed to Laplace. If an experiment has a finite number of equally likely outcomes and f outcomes are favorable to event A, the probability is P(A) = f / N, where N is the total number of outcomes.

Examples include ideal dice, coins, and simple card games, under the assumption of symmetry. The main limitation is that it presupposes equiprobability and a finite sample space, which is not always realistic or available (continuous spaces, complex physical systems, …).

1.2 Frequentist Interpretation

The frequentist view defines probability as the long-run relative frequency of an event:

P(A) ≈ Kn(A) / n, where Kn(A) is the number of occurrences of A in n independent repetitions.

Intuitively, as n grows large, the ratio Kₙ(A)/n converges to a stable value that we call P(A). This is formalized by the Law of Large Numbers (see HMWK 4). The frequentist approach is very natural for repeatable experiments, but less so for unique events (elections, singular catastrophes, …) and it treats probability as a limit concept, which is not directly observable.

1.3 Bayesian Interpretation

The Bayesian interpretation views probability as a measure of degree of belief (or plausibility) of a proposition, given the available information. Probabilities are thus subjective or epistemic, and they are updated as new evidence arrives via Bayes’ theorem:

P(H | D) = [P(D | H) · P(H)] / P(D)

Here H is a hypothesis and D is the data. Bayesian probability applies even to unique events and directly incorporates prior knowledge. The criticism is that priors may appear arbitrary or subjective; however, the framework is internally consistent and extremely powerful in statistical inference.

1.4 Geometric Interpretation

The geometric (or continuous) interpretation arises when the sample space is a continuum (e.g. points on a segment or in a region). Probabilities are defined via ratios of lengths, areas, or volumes. For example, if a point is chosen uniformly at random in a disk, the probability that it falls in a given sub-region is the area of the sub-region divided by the area of the disk.

This interpretation generalizes the classical one from counting finite outcomes to measuring sets, but it relies implicitly on the notion of a measure (e.g. Lebesgue measure on ℝⁿ).

1.5 Axiomatic Interpretation

The axiomatic approach, due to Kolmogorov (1933), does not attempt to define what probability “is” philosophically. Instead, it specifies how a probability function must behave. The starting point is a triple:

(Ω, 𝓕, P)

  • Ω — sample space (all possible outcomes);
  • 𝓕 — σ-algebra of events (measurable subsets of Ω);
  • P — function 𝓕 → [0,1] satisfying the axioms.

The axioms are:

  1. Non-negativity: for all A ∈ 𝓕, P(A) ≥ 0.
  2. Normalization: P(Ω) = 1.
  3. Countable additivity: if (Aᵢ) is a sequence of pairwise disjoint events, then P(⋃ Aᵢ) = ∑ P(Aᵢ).

Classical, frequentist, Bayesian, and geometric probabilities can all be seen as models that produce a specific triple (Ω, 𝓕, P) satisfying these axioms. This is the basis for the reconciliation discussed next.

2) How the Axiomatic Approach Reconciles Interpretations

2.1 Unifying Different Notions of Probability

At a philosophical level, the classical, frequentist, and Bayesian interpretations may disagree on what P(A) means (symmetry, long-run frequency, degree of belief, …). However, at a mathematical level, they all propose numerical values in [0,1] for events (subsets of Ω) and these values should obey some intuitive rules (e.g., probability of the union of disjoint events is the sum).

Kolmogorov’s axioms abstract away the philosophical debate and require only that the assigned probabilities obey a consistent algebra on events. A classical model on a finite Ω, a frequentist limit model on an infinite sequence of trials, a Bayesian posterior model given data, and a geometric model on a continuous space all fit as long as they respect non-negativity, normalization, and countable additivity.

2.2 Classical vs Frequentist vs Bayesian

  • A classical model can be seen as choosing Ω finite with the counting measure and then defining P(A) = |A| / |Ω|. This clearly satisfies the axioms.
  • A frequentist model interprets P(A) as the limit of frequencies in an infinite sequence of i.i.d. trials; the limit (if it exists) automatically obeys the axioms thanks to the LLN and the underlying construction of product measures.
  • A Bayesian model takes Ω to be a parameter space or outcome space, and P represents a prior or posterior measure on Ω. Bayes’ theorem is just the Radon–Nikodym derivative (change-of-measure formula) in this setting.

From the axiomatic perspective, all three are simply different ways of constructing a probability measure; the derived theorems and properties are the same.

2.3 Geometric and Continuous Models

The geometric interpretation is formalized by letting Ω be a subset of ℝⁿ, 𝓕 the σ-algebra of measurable sets, and P a probability measure absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure (e.g., uniform on a segment, normal distribution on ℝ). The axioms ensure that probabilities behave nicely under limits, countable unions, and intersections, avoiding paradoxes from naive area/volume arguments.

2.4 Resolving Conceptual Tensions

The axiomatic framework does not choose one interpretation as “true”; instead it:

  • provides a common language (events, measures, random variables);
  • separates the construction of probability models from their mathematical properties;
  • shows that, as long as the axioms hold, all interpretations share the same logical consequences (LLN, CLT, Bayes theorem, inclusion–exclusion, …).

3) Probability Theory as a Special Case of Measure Theory

3.1 σ-Algebras of Events

In measure theory, we start from a set Ω and a collection 𝓕 of subsets satisfying:

  • Ω ∈ 𝓕 and ∅ ∈ 𝓕;
  • if A ∈ 𝓕 then its complement Ac is in 𝓕;
  • if A₁, A₂, … ∈ 𝓕 then ⋃ Aᵢ ∈ 𝓕 (and therefore also ⋂ Aᵢ ∈ 𝓕).

Such a collection 𝓕 is called a σ-algebra. In probability, each A ∈ 𝓕 is an event whose probability P(A) is defined.

3.2 Probability Measures

A measure μ on (Ω, 𝓕) is a function μ: 𝓕 → [0, ∞] satisfying:

  • μ(∅) = 0;
  • for disjoint Aᵢ, μ(⋃ Aᵢ) = ∑ μ(Aᵢ).

A probability measure is just a measure with total mass 1, i.e. μ(Ω) = 1. Therefore, probability theory is a special case of measure theory where we restrict to measures with total mass one and interpret them as probabilities.

3.3 Measurable Functions and Random Variables

In measure theory, a function X: Ω → ℝ is measurable if for every Borel set B ⊆ ℝ, the preimage X⁻¹(B) = { ω ∈ Ω : X(ω) ∈ B } belongs to 𝓕.

In probability, a random variable is precisely such a measurable function. The distribution of X is the pushforward measure:

PX(B) = P( X ∈ B ) = P( X⁻¹(B) )

This definition works uniformly for discrete, continuous, and mixed distributions, and for vector-valued random variables. Expectations and variances are defined as measure-theoretic integrals (e.g., E[X] = ∫ X dP), providing a unified and rigorous framework.

3.4 Why Measure Theory Matters

Measure theory guarantees:

  • consistent handling of infinite sample spaces and limits of random variables;
  • a clean formulation of convergence notions (almost sure, in probability, in L², …);
  • strong theorems such as LLN and CLT under general conditions;
  • a common language for probability, integration, and functional analysis.

In summary, probability theory is measure theory with an interpretative layer: measures represent probabilities and measurable functions represent random quantities.

4) Subadditivity from the Axioms

A useful consequence of the axioms is the subadditivity property:

P(A ∪ B) ≤ P(A) + P(B)

for any events A and B in 𝓕. I derive it step by step from the axioms.

4.1 Decomposition of the Union

We can decompose A ∪ B into disjoint parts:

  • A ∩ Bc (only A occurs);
  • B (all outcomes where B occurs).

Indeed:

A ∪ B = (A ∩ Bc) ∪ B,

and the two sets on the right-hand side are disjoint. By countable additivity (applied to two sets), we obtain:

P(A ∪ B) = P(A ∩ Bc) + P(B).

4.2 Bounding by P(A) + P(B)

Notice that A can be written as:

A = (A ∩ Bc) ∪ (A ∩ B),

which is a union of disjoint sets. Thus:

P(A) = P(A ∩ Bc) + P(A ∩ B) ≥ P(A ∩ Bc),

because P(A ∩ B) ≥ 0 by non-negativity. Combining this inequality with the previous identity:

P(A ∪ B) = P(A ∩ Bc) + P(B) ≤ P(A) + P(B).

This proves subadditivity for two events. The general subadditivity for countable unions follows by a more advanced argument using disjointification and countable additivity.

5) Inclusion–Exclusion Principle from the Axioms

5.1 Formula for Two Events

For two events A and B, the inclusion–exclusion formula is:

P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B).

This can also be derived directly from the axioms.

5.2 Derivation

Start again from the decomposition:

A = (A ∩ Bc) ∪ (A ∩ B),   with disjoint union.

Hence:

P(A) = P(A ∩ Bc) + P(A ∩ B).

From Section 4 we also have:

P(A ∪ B) = P(A ∩ Bc) + P(B).

Solve the first equation for P(A ∩ Bc):

P(A ∩ Bc) = P(A) − P(A ∩ B).

Substitute into the second:

P(A ∪ B) = [P(A) − P(A ∩ B)] + P(B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B).

This is exactly the inclusion–exclusion principle for two events, obtained by combining only:

  • set identities (decompositions of A and A ∪ B);
  • countable additivity on disjoint unions;
  • non-negativity (implicitly to justify some inequalities).

5.3 Intuitive Interpretation

The formula says: if we simply add P(A) and P(B), we double-count outcomes where both A and B happen (the intersection). To correct this, we subtract P(A ∩ B). This matches the Venn diagram intuition and shows how the axioms formalize a very natural counting logic.

6) Takeaways

  • Different interpretations of probability (classical, frequentist, Bayesian, geometric) disagree on the meaning of P(A), but all can be realized as models of an axiomatic probability space.
  • The axiomatic approach (Kolmogorov) provides a unified and consistent framework based on a triple (Ω, 𝓕, P), with events as elements of a σ-algebra and P as a probability measure.
  • Measure theory underlies modern probability: a probability measure is just a normalized measure, and random variables are measurable functions whose distributions and expectations are defined via integrals.
  • Important properties like subadditivity and inclusion–exclusion are not extra assumptions but direct consequences of the basic axioms plus simple set-theoretic identities.
