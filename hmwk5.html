HMWK 5 — Measures of Location

Measures of Location (Various Averages)

Goal

In this homework I review the main measures of location (central tendency) and explain what they are, how they are computed, and when they make sense. Different “averages” exist because real datasets differ (skewness, outliers, scales, ratios). Choosing the right average improves interpretation and downstream decisions.

Key Averages

1) Arithmetic Mean

For values \(x_1,\dots,x_n\), the arithmetic mean is \( \bar{x}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i \). It is the default choice when data are roughly symmetric and measured on an interval/ratio scale. It is not robust: even a single extreme outlier can shift it a lot.

Use when: errors average out, distribution is not heavy–tailed, and you want an additive notion of “typical”.

2) Median

Order the data; the median is the middle value (or the mean of the two central values if \(n\) is even). It is robust to outliers and appropriate for skewed distributions or ordinal data. In income analysis, the median income is often more representative than the mean.

Use when: skewness or outliers are present; you need robustness and interpretability.

3) Mode

The mode is the most frequent value. It is the only measure that works for nominal/categorical data (e.g., most common brand). For continuous data it may be unstable and not unique.

Use when: categories are involved, or clear peaks exist in discrete counts.

4) Geometric Mean

For positive values, \( \mathrm{GM}=(\prod_{i=1}^n x_i)^{1/n} \). It is the right notion of “average” for multiplicative quantities such as growth factors, returns, or ratios on a log scale. Not defined if any value ≤ 0.

Use when: compounding/growth is central (e.g., “average” percentage growth across years).

5) Harmonic Mean

For positive values, \( \mathrm{HM}=\dfrac{n}{\sum_{i=1}^n 1/x_i} \). It appropriately averages rates and reciprocals, giving more weight to small values. Classic example: average speed over equal distances.

Use when: averaging rates/ratios where denominators matter (cost per unit, speed, precision scores).

6) Weighted Mean

If items have weights \(w_i\) (importance, reliability, sample sizes), \( \bar{x}_w=\dfrac{\sum w_i x_i}{\sum w_i} \). It generalizes the arithmetic mean and is essential in meta-analysis or stratified data.

7) Trimmed / Winsorized Mean

A trimmed mean discards a small fraction of the lowest and highest observations (e.g., 10%) before computing the mean; a Winsorized mean replaces extremes with the nearest remaining values. Both improve robustness while keeping an additive interpretation.

Use when: you want a compromise between mean (efficient) and median (robust).

Which Average When? (Quick Guide)

  • Symmetric, no outliers → Arithmetic mean.
  • Skewed or with outliers → Median (or trimmed/Winsorized mean).
  • Nominal categories → Mode.
  • Multiplicative effects / growth → Geometric mean.
  • Rates/ratios with equal-distance denominators → Harmonic mean.
  • Different importance/precision → Weighted mean.

Mini Examples

  • Income: heavy right tail → median is more representative than mean.
  • Annual returns: 1.10, 1.05, 0.90 → GM captures compound “typical” growth.
  • Two equal road segments: 60 km/h then 30 km/h → the correct average speed is the HM, not the AM.

Measures of Dispersion

While measures of location describe the “center” of a dataset, measures of dispersion quantify how spread out the values are. They are essential to interpret the stability or variability of the data around its mean.

  • Range: difference between the largest and smallest value. Simple but highly sensitive to outliers.
  • Variance (σ²): average of squared deviations from the mean; indicates how far values typically lie from the center. Expressed in squared units.
  • Standard deviation (σ): square root of variance, restoring the original measurement scale; widely used to express volatility or noise.
  • Interquartile range (IQR): difference between the 75th and 25th percentiles; robust measure of spread, not affected by extreme values.
  • Mean absolute deviation (MAD): average of absolute deviations; a more interpretable, robust alternative to variance.

In practice: small dispersion means data points cluster tightly around the mean (low variability); large dispersion means high variability or uncertainty. Both location and dispersion are required to describe a distribution completely.

HMWK 5 — Online (Recurrence) Formulas

Online Mean & Variance — Recurrence Formulas + Minimal Code

Goal

I derive the incremental (online) update rules for the mean and the variance. The idea is to update statistics when a new value xn arrives, without storing the whole dataset.

Derivation

1) Mean (easy)

Let n−1 points have mean mn−1. When a new value xn arrives:

mn = mn−1 + (xn − mn−1)/n

This is just an algebraic rearrangement of the definition of the mean.

2) Variance (numerically stable update)

Define M2 as the running sum of squared deviations from the mean: M2n = Σi=1..n(xi − mn)². When the new sample xn arrives:

  1. n ← n−1 + 1
  2. δ = xn − mn−1
  3. mn = mn−1 + δ / n
  4. δ₂ = xn − mn
  5. M2n = M2n−1 + δ · δ₂

Then:

  • Population variance (biased): σ² = M2 / n
  • Sample variance (unbiased): s² = M2 / (n − 1) (for n ≥ 2)

These recurrences are the classic Welford/Knuth online updates; they avoid catastrophic cancellation of the naïve one-pass formula.

Why Online Methods? (Quick Computational Note)

  • Time per update: O(1) per new sample (vs. O(n) if recomputing from scratch).
  • Memory: O(1) state (n, mean, M2) — no need to store the whole stream.
  • Numerical stability: Welford/Knuth updates avoid catastrophic cancellation of the naïve one-pass formula.
  • Scalability: Pairwise/merge formulas allow parallel/stream processing and later merging of partial stats.

Optional — Merge / Pairwise (for parallel streams)

Given two partial aggregates A and B with (n, mean, M2), let δ = meanB − meanA, n = nA + nB, then:
mean = (nA·meanA + nB·meanB) / n
M2 = M2A + M2B + δ² · nA·nB / n

Experimental Results

Test A — Exact sequence [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]

The online algorithm reproduces the theoretical statistics exactly: mean = 3, σ² = 2, s² = 2.5. This confirms the recurrence formulas are algebraically consistent with the definition of the mean and variance.

Test B — Numerical-stability stress (1e9 ± 0.001)

For large offsets with tiny deviations, the online algorithm remained stable: mean ≈ 1 000 000 000, σ² ≈ 1 × 10⁻⁶, s² ≈ 1 × 10⁻⁶. No loss of precision was observed — the values are exact within floating-point rounding, proving that the recurrence formulation avoids catastrophic cancellation that affects naive one-pass formulas.

Both experiments confirm the correctness and numerical robustness of the online (Welford) updates.

Optional Discussion — Advantages of Online Algorithms

From a computational and numerical analysis perspective, online algorithms provide significant advantages over traditional batch methods for statistical updates. Instead of reprocessing the entire dataset each time a new observation arrives, they maintain and update a small fixed state — typically just n, mean, and M2.

1) Numerical stability

Online updates compute differences relative to the current mean rather than large cumulative sums. This minimizes round-off errors and prevents catastrophic cancellation — a problem that occurs when subtracting nearly equal large numbers in the naïve one-pass formula (E[x²] − E[x]²). By maintaining deviations incrementally, the Welford method remains accurate even for very large or very small datasets.

2) Error propagation and overflow

Because the algorithm never accumulates huge intermediate sums, the numerical range of intermediate values remains small. This dramatically reduces the risk of overflow and mitigates the propagation of rounding errors across iterations. It is therefore particularly well-suited for long data streams or high-precision computations in constrained environments (e.g., sensors or embedded systems).

3) Computational efficiency and scalability

Each update requires constant time and memory (O(1)), making the algorithm efficient for real-time or streaming applications. In distributed systems, partial aggregates can be merged using the pairwise formulas of Chan–Golub–LeVeque (1979), enabling scalable parallel computation without recomputing from raw data.

4) Robustness and practical impact

In practice, these properties make online algorithms robust against precision loss and data-size growth. They are widely adopted in machine learning, data stream monitoring, and cybersecurity analytics, where statistics must be updated continuously under limited memory and with minimal numerical drift.

In summary, online (incremental) statistics are not just faster; they are also safer numerically, more memory-efficient, and more adaptable to real-world data flows.
